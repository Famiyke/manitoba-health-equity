# Health Equity Brief
## Manitoba Regional Health Authority Analysis
**Prepared for:** Manitoba Health Planning Committee
**Data source:** Manitoba Health Annual Statistics 2022–23 | gov.mb.ca/health/annstats
**Analysis date:** 2024

---

## Purpose of This Brief

This brief summarises a cross-RHA analysis of nine health indicators across Manitoba's five Regional Health Authorities. It identifies where the equity gaps are most severe, explains what is driving them, and recommends three specific investments.

---

## The Equity Landscape in Manitoba

Manitoba has five RHAs of vastly different sizes, geographies, and population health profiles. Winnipeg serves 780,000 people in an urban setting with reasonable health system access. Northern RHA serves 75,000 people across a remote, predominantly Indigenous population with severely limited access.

The data tells a consistent story: every negative health outcome is worst in the Northern RHA, and every positive health indicator is lowest there. This is not a coincidence. It is the result of decades of underinvestment in primary care, income supports, and social infrastructure in northern Manitoba.

---

## The Three Most Important Numbers

**669** — premature mortality per 100,000 in Northern RHA. The provincial average is 339. Northern residents are dying young at twice the rate of other Manitobans.

**41.5%** — proportion of Northern RHA residents with no regular doctor. Four in ten people in northern Manitoba have no primary care relationship. When they get sick, they go to emergency departments or go without care entirely.

**38.6%** — proportion of Northern RHA population living in low income. This is more than double Winnipeg's rate (18.2%) and is the single strongest predictor of poor health outcomes in the data.

## What is Driving the Gaps

### Primary care access is the mechanism

The no-doctor rate is the most direct driver of the health gap. When people have no regular physician, they cannot manage chronic conditions, cannot access preventive care, and cannot get prescriptions renewed. They rely on emergency departments for conditions that should be handled in primary care — which is more expensive and less effective.

The correlation between no-doctor rate and mortality is r = 0.93. This is not statistical noise. It is a structural relationship.

### Low income shapes everything

Low income correlates r = 0.94 with mortality rate across all five RHAs. This is the strongest correlation in the dataset. Income determines housing quality, food security, ability to take time off work to seek care, and ability to afford prescription co-payments.

The Northern RHA has the highest low-income rate in the province at 38.6%. Any health intervention that does not also address the income and housing conditions of Northern communities will have limited impact.

### Diabetes is a system failure, not a behaviour problem

Northern RHA diabetes prevalence (19.6%) is twice Winnipeg's (9.7%). High diabetes rates in remote Indigenous communities are well-documented in the literature and are linked to food access, exercise infrastructure, and the intergenerational health effects of colonisation — not individual choices.

Treating diabetes at the individual level through medications costs more than preventing it through community-level nutrition programs. Manitoba has evidence-based models from other provinces that demonstrate this.

## Three Investments That Would Make a Difference

### 1. Place-based primary care teams in northern communities

**What:** Fund full-time, multi-disciplinary primary care teams (physician, nurse practitioner, community health worker, mental health worker) in each of the 8 largest northern communities.

**Why now:** The no-doctor rate has been above 40% for five consecutive years. Incentive programs have not worked. Only structural placement commitments with multi-year contracts and loan forgiveness will change this.

**Estimated impact:** A 10% reduction in no-doctor rate in Northern RHA would reduce emergency department visits by an estimated 8–12% and improve chronic disease management for approximately 7,500 patients.

### 2. Community health worker diabetes prevention program

**Who:** Northern RHA and Prairie Mountain Health — both above 10% diabetes prevalence.

**What:** Community health workers embedded in schools, recreation centres, and community buildings delivering nutrition literacy, physical activity programming, and diabetes screening.

**Why this works:** Published evidence from comparable Canadian programs (James Bay Cree, Northern Ontario) shows a 15–20% reduction in new diabetes cases within 5 years at a cost of approximately $800 per participant — compared to $15,000+ per year for a managed diabetes patient.

### 3. Income and housing investment as a health intervention

**What:** Manitoba Health should formally advocate for and co-fund targeted income support programs (basic income pilots, housing subsidies) in Northern RHA communities, framed as health interventions with health outcome tracking.

**Why:** The correlation data makes this unavoidable. A health ministry that spends $500M on Northern health care while the poverty rate stays at 38% is treating symptoms, not causes. Framing income support as a health investment changes the funding calculus.

## What This Analysis Cannot Determine

- Whether RHA-level gaps have worsened or improved over longer time periods (data covers 2018–2022 only)
- Patient-level outcomes and pathways through the health system
- Cost-effectiveness estimates without access to provincial administrative health data

More detailed analysis would require access to Manitoba's Population Health Research Data Repository (PHRDP) under a formal data sharing agreement.
